\section{Introduction}

\begin{frame}{Why This Matters in Corporate Finance}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Earnings disclosures matter because they update expectations about future cash flows and therefore firm value.
  \item If markets are efficient, stock prices should adjust quickly when the disclosure contains genuinely new information.
  \item This makes earnings surprise a useful setting for linking disclosure, valuation, and market efficiency.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Research Question and Prediction}
\begin{block}{Core Question}
Do firms with more positive earnings surprise experience better short-run abnormal returns around the disclosure date in China A-shares?
\end{block}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Prediction: more positive surprise should be associated with more positive abnormal return.
  \item We focus on short-run price adjustment rather than long-run drift.
  \item The goal is a clean empirical test, not a forced strong headline.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Sample and Empirical Setup}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Our headline sample focuses on earnings preannouncements, because they are the cleanest event type for isolating the disclosure shock.
  \item Other earnings-related events are useful comparisons, but they mix different information environments and weaken interpretation.
  \item We examine abnormal returns over short windows from 3 to 20 trading days after the event.
\end{itemize}
\begin{block}{Why this design is narrower}
A narrower sample is less likely to give an inflated headline result, but it is more credible for a valuation-based interpretation.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How We Measure Earnings Surprise}
\small
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
  \item We benchmark the disclosure against analyst expectations matched as closely as possible to the same reporting period.
  \item We compare three surprise lenses: level, percentage, and standardized surprise.
  \item The estimated relation can look different when surprise is scaled differently.
\end{itemize}
\begin{block}{Interpretation rule}
Standardized surprise is a robustness view, not the only headline definition.
\end{block}

\begin{frame}{Main Result: Directional but Modest Evidence}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The cleanest sample shows the expected direction: firms with higher surprise generally outperform firms with lower surprise after the disclosure.
  \item This is consistent with short-run valuation adjustment.
  \item But the visual separation is not strong enough, by itself, to justify an aggressive headline claim.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Results Depend on Specification Choices}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.40\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The estimated effect changes when we change how surprise is scaled and how tightly expectations are matched.
  \item That sensitivity is economically important because it tells us the result is not fully robust.
  \item So the right conclusion is disciplined and cautious, not headline-driven.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{What We Learn About Valuation and Efficiency}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The cleaner baseline is broadly consistent with the idea that earnings disclosures affect valuation in the short run.
  \item At the same time, the evidence is not strong enough to claim a large or universal inefficiency story.
  \item A more defensible interpretation is that the market response exists, but measured strength depends heavily on how expectations are constructed.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why We Keep the Conclusion Cautious}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The strongest headline result is weak rather than decisive.
  \item Inference changes when surprise measurement, expectation matching, or event composition changes.
  \item That means empirical discipline matters as much as statistical significance in this setting.
\end{itemize}
\begin{block}{Bottom line}
The project is more convincing as a careful corporate-finance empirical exercise than as proof of a strong market-inefficiency headline.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conclusion}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Earnings-related disclosures do appear to matter for valuation in the cleaner preannouncement sample.
  \item The evidence is directionally supportive, but modest and sensitive to measurement choices.
  \item Our main takeaway is a cautious one: this is suggestive evidence on disclosure and market efficiency, not a definitive headline result.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
